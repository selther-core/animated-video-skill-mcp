# Cross-niche examples: scripts, storyboards and voice cues

These are original *small production examples* illustrating how the same reference-inspired visual grammar can serve different niches. They are not reference footage, approved claims, or finished video projects. **All narrator and character voices use an American accent**; select U.S. English (`en-US`) voices for these English scripts. All voices should be energetic, well-projected, clear and expressive—not boring—and prominent in the mix without shouting or clipping. See `../references/voice-direction.md` for niche-specific voice types, detailed delivery and more TTS-ready scripts. Estimated cue times are a planning scaffold: generate the speech, measure it, then move scene boundaries to match. Adapt titles, characters, assets, safety guidance and facts to the brief. Final publishing requires a complete, checked deliverable.

## 1. Fictional business case — the bakery queue

**Brief:** Niche: small-business storytelling. Audience: curious general viewers. Intent: entertain and illustrate a tradeoff, not advise a real business. Length: ~35–45 seconds. Tone: warm, lightly humorous. The bakery, people and events are fictional.

**Hook and thumbnail:** A tired baker faces an enormous queue; original title text “TOO MANY CUSTOMERS?” on a simplified shop exterior. No numerical earnings or real-brand claims.

**Cast and assets:** Mina, a baker with a green apron; one customer with a rust-red scarf; original queue silhouettes; café counter, order cards, bread tray and a small bell. Side-on bakery set with a warm window and a cool street outside.

| Cue | Estimated visual | TTS-ready text |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00–0:08 | Wide shot: a queue grows from two people to a line; Mina blinks and looks at her empty tray. | “Mina's little bakery had a problem most owners dream about. People kept showing up. But every new order made the line move slower.” |
| 0:08–0:18 | Close crop on counter. Two order cards overlap; a single hand reaches for both. Cut to a short customer reaction. | “She could rush every loaf, or she could change the way orders were taken. One busy morning, she tried a simple idea.” |
| 0:18–0:30 | Split the counter into two clearly labeled areas: “order” and “collect.” Show a card pass from one hand to another; the line begins moving. | “She separated ordering from pickup. Now one person could choose while another collected a finished bag. The queue didn't vanish, but it became easier to follow.” |
| 0:30–0:40 | Wide shot returns; Mina smiles, customer exits. | “It's a fictional bakery, but the question is real: when work piles up, which small change would make the next step clearer?” |

**Voice and sound:** American-accented adult narrator, medium-low pitch and warm, lightly textured tone; energetic, conversational and playful at about 140–150 wpm. Pause before the change and avoid an ad-read. A single low-volume door bell and paper shuffle are optional original sounds. Don't assert that this intervention would work for every actual shop. Deliver an original thumbnail, script, captions and evidence/rights notes.

## 2. Personal productivity — a desk reset

**Brief:** Niche: practical productivity. Audience: people overwhelmed by tasks. Intent: a short, non-medical suggestion; avoid guarantees. Length: ~30–40 seconds. Mood: calm and encouraging.

**Hook and thumbnail:** Close-up of a desk buried in slips of paper and a worried character; original wording “WHERE TO START?”

**Cast and assets:** One original character, a desk, loose cards, pencil and three labeled zones. Warm desk lamp against a cool background. No classroom or scientific diagram is required.

| Cue | Estimated visual | TTS-ready text |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00–0:08 | Stack of paper grows around character; head turns from one task to another. | “When every task seems urgent, even choosing the first one can feel like work.” |
| 0:08–0:19 | Hand pushes cards into three areas; labels appear sequentially. | “Try putting your tasks into three small groups: do soon, plan later, and let go. The labels don't have to be perfect.” |
| 0:19–0:29 | Character circles one task and picks up a pencil; camera moves closer. | “Pick one action you can actually begin, and make its first step specific. Not ‘organize everything’—just ‘sort the notes on this desk.’” |
| 0:29–0:37 | Wider quiet frame, one card separated from the pile. | “This won't solve every deadline. It can give you a clearer place to start. What would your first card say?” |

**Voice and sound:** American-accented adult narrator, medium pitch and bright, clear tone; energetic and compassionate at about 120–135 wpm, without whispering or promising results. Optional quiet paper sounds. Avoid claims that the method cures anxiety, guarantees productivity or replaces professional care.

## 3. Fictional animal story — the lost kite

**Brief:** Niche: family-friendly animal adventure. Intent: a character-led short story with a clear resolution. Length: ~35–45 seconds. Fictional animals; no behavioral claim is implied.

**Hook and thumbnail:** A small original fox reaches toward a red kite high in a tree; original wording “HOW WILL IT GET DOWN?”

**Cast and assets:** Fox with pointed muzzle, ears, tail and four separately pivoted legs; small bird with head, body, wings and feet; kite, string, tree, grass and path. Start from the same simplified, softly lit visual family but design animals as animals, not humans in costume.

| Cue | Estimated visual | TTS-ready text |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00–0:08 | Establish clearing. Fox looks up; a kite is caught in a branch. | “A fox found a kite tangled high in a tree. It wasn't his, but somebody had clearly lost it.” |
| 0:08–0:18 | Fox jumps and lands short. A bird perches on the branch and looks down. | “He tried to jump, but the branch was too high. A bird nearby noticed the string and had a better idea.” |
| 0:18–0:30 | Bird lifts the loose string, fox holds the lower end; the kite comes free and floats down. | “The bird tugged at the loose end while the fox held the string below. Together they worked it free, gently enough to keep the kite in one piece.” |
| 0:30–0:40 | A child-shaped silhouette arrives at the path; fox leaves the kite in view. Warm light closes the scene. | “Soon a child came looking. The fox left the kite beside the path, and the bird watched from the tree. Mystery solved.” |

**Voice:** American-accented adult storyteller, medium-to-bright pitch, warm and lively at about 125–145 wpm; let the problem feel curious, not frightening.

**Animation and sound:** Create a quadruped rig with shoulder/hip/leg pivots and a tail; move the bird with simple wing poses. Keep feet and beak in contact with the string when pulling. Suggested original wind and foliage sounds; no required spoken dialogue. This is a fictional narrative, not evidence of animal behavior.

## 4. Historical / educational — first Moon landing

**Brief:** Niche: history. Audience: general. Intent: brief factual account. Length: ~30–40 seconds. Respectful, curious tone. Source starting point: NASA's [Apollo 11 mission page](https://www.nasa.gov/mission/apollo-11/); verify all statements against current authoritative sources before publishing.

**Hook and thumbnail:** Small suited figure on a stylized lunar landscape with a ladder behind; original wording “ONE STEP, BIG MOMENT.” Avoid using NASA logos, photos or footage without checking rights.

**Cast and assets:** Original stylized astronaut with helmet, suit, backpack and leg joints; simple lunar module silhouette, lunar ground, mission date card and Earth in the distance. This is a stylized illustration, not an accurate technical diagram.

| Cue | Estimated visual | TTS-ready text |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00–0:07 | Moon surface at night; a tiny original figure stands beside a ladder. | “In July 1969, a crew traveled farther from home than most people could imagine.” |
| 0:07–0:19 | Simple mission map Earth → Moon; date label. | “Apollo 11 carried Neil Armstrong, Buzz Aldrin and Michael Collins to the Moon. Armstrong and Aldrin descended to the surface while Collins remained in lunar orbit.” |
| 0:19–0:30 | Slow descent down ladder, boot contacts ground; another character remains near the lander. | “Armstrong became the first person to step onto the lunar surface. Aldrin followed, and the two astronauts explored the landing area.” |
| 0:30–0:38 | Pull back to the small craft and distant Earth; caption “July 1969.” | “Their visit was brief, but it became one of the defining images of the space age.” |

**Voice and sound:** American-accented adult narrator, medium or medium-low pitch, clear, engaged and expressively awed at about 125–140 wpm. Leave space around names and dates; do not imitate historical voices. Optional original abstract ambient bed. Do not invent quotations or reproduce copyrighted recordings. Verify dates, crew roles, description and all illustration rights.

## How to turn any example into a production

- Replace the example's subject, script, figures and visual metaphors with the user's actual niche. Determine whether the requested work is factual, fictional or a blend; validate claims accordingly.
- Record a unique cue ID for every line and visual event, synthesize voices with available native TTS, and adjust timings from actual audio. Include captions, original or licensed sounds, an original thumbnail, title, description, sources if factual, and a playlist recommendation based on channel context.
- Render, review and fix against `../SKILL.md`; report any missing visual/audio review honestly. The example cues and images alone are not a finished video.
