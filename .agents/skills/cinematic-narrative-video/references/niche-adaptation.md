# Adapting the video style to any niche

This skill is about a **visual and editorial language**, not a subject category. It can serve fiction, storytelling, history, business, psychology, education, sports, travel, technology, personal growth, comedy, gaming, animal stories, biographies, finance, health, or another niche. Choose the narrative form, characters and scene design that fit the user's topic. Do not force every project into a science lesson, classroom, chalkboard, genetic diagram, numerical counter, or hypothetical question. The fictional WWII cut is one project-authored example, not a required setting, genre or script template.

## 1. Separate transferable style from topic-specific content

**Transferable elements** illustrated by the project-authored WWII example: compact, expressive simplified figures; legible side-on or front-facing settings; small characters against larger environments; controlled, economical movement; readable faces and gestures; warm/cool contrast; atmospheric gradients and vignettes; clearly staged props; small labels where useful; purposeful changes between a concrete scene and a simplified explanatory or metaphorical scene; reuse of sets; reaction moments; and visual progression within longer held scenes. Use a clear focal point where useful; thumbnail style should fit the actual topic. See `ww2-example-breakdown.md` for example evidence and limitations.

**Change for the niche:** subject, characters, clothing, animals, setting, objects, colors, historical/real-world accuracy, diagrams, narration, humor, dialogue, structure, sound and runtime. Select illustration motifs that carry the story. A small-town business narrative might use a counter, order tickets and a storefront; a ghost story might use hallways, doors, lamplight and shadows; a sports story might use a scoreboard, field, chalk arrows and a player's decisions. These are proposed adaptations, not footage from the example film.

## 2. Brief → creative treatment

Before writing a script, record:

```
Niche / topic:
Audience and what they already know:
Primary intent: explain / teach / persuade / entertain / tell a story / comment / compare / other
Desired emotion and tone:
Promise or central conflict:
Appropriate narrative form:
Real-world claims requiring verification, or fiction label:
Main figures / animals and their goals:
Three relevant locations and four recognisable props:
A visual metaphor or diagram only if it genuinely helps:
User's format, language, length, brand and content boundaries:
Audience-safe content, accuracy and rights constraints:
```

Resolve any essential ambiguity with a focused question. Otherwise make reasonable assumptions and proceed. Within each project keep an appropriate, consistent style bible for its actual subjects and lighting; create a fresh one when the niche or brief calls for it. Never make the same cast, set or opening mandatory across unrelated niches.

## Adapt an unfamiliar or mixed niche

For a subject not in the table: (1) extract the viewer's desired outcome and domain constraints from the prompt and intake; (2) identify concrete actions, locations, subjects, objects and useful visual evidence from reliable sources or fiction rules; (3) choose a structure and audiovisual vocabulary based on that outcome, not on the nearest existing example; (4) draft 2–3 candidate ways to show each key beat and select by clarity, fidelity, audience, feasibility and the user's direction; (5) verify domain-specific facts, rights, risks, platform and cultural context; (6) test a representative moment and revise. Mix structures for hybrid niches when needed. If the video has no people, omit wardrobe/rig and animate the relevant objects, interfaces, maps, environments or diagrams instead. If it has no speech, omit TTS.

## 3. Pick a story pattern and map it to shots

| Intended kind | Reliable starting structure | Example visual vocabulary | Research / content checks |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tutorial / how-to | outcome → common obstacle → steps demonstrated → result → recap | workbench, device screen, hands, arrows, progress indicators | verify steps against current versions; test instructions; safety warnings when warranted |
| Business / case study | context → decision → tradeoff → consequences → takeaway | shop counter, receipts, map, customer flow, simple invented charts if explicitly labeled | credible source for real firms, dates, revenue and causation; distinguish a fictional example |
| History / biography | time/place → people and motivation → turning point → documented outcome → context | period-appropriate street, documents, rooms, silhouettes, dates | cross-check chronology, attribution, contested claims and image rights; do not invent quotes |
| Fiction / comedy | desire → obstacle → escalating attempts → twist or choice → satisfying resolution | recurring cast, props with clear functions, staging for visual gags | clearly indicate fiction; avoid misleading real-person claims and unlicensed characters |
| Personal growth / psychology | relatable situation → tension → practical idea → example → caveat / next step | desk, calendar, character choices, time cards, simple visual metaphors | avoid overstated universal results; distinguish evidence from advice; no diagnosis |
| Finance / economics | concrete decision → options → assumptions → comparison → risks | receipts, wallet, simplified ledger, scales, icons | use current and jurisdiction-specific sources; disclose assumptions and uncertainty; no promises of returns or personalized financial directives |
| Health / wellbeing | audience concern → context → evidence / safety → practical general guidance → professional support when relevant | character, labels, health-related props without graphic imagery | high-quality medical sources, measured claims, appropriate caution; avoid diagnosis or treatment instructions beyond capability |
| Sports / games | goal → challenge → key decision or move → result → reflection | field, board, positions, movement paths, scoreboard | verify real results and quotes; use original art and rights-cleared footage; don't misrepresent a real match |
| Animal story / nature | animal's goal → environment → obstacle → behavior → outcome | species-readable animal silhouette, habitat, nests, food, scale cues | check species behavior if factual; no invented natural-history claims |
| Commentary / opinion | fair context → thesis → supporting reasons → counterpoint → conclusion | contrasting scenarios, text cards, reactions | distinguish opinion from factual claims; avoid fabricated evidence or misleading quotes |

These are options, not rigid formulas. Follow the user's niche and format if it differs. Ensure stakes and payoff match the title and thumbnail. Some stories require no narration and some need dialogue; the voice and audio layout must follow the chosen form.

## 4. Scene design by meaning, not by a fixed list

1. Extract 4–8 meaningful story beats for a short project or more for a long one. For each, write a function: *introduce character*, *show a problem*, *demonstrate a step*, *compare alternatives*, *reveal a consequence* or *resolve a conflict*.
2. Build as many reusable sets as the niche and story need, for example: e.g. bakery interior + sidewalk + order board; or forest clearing + den + tree canopy. Use original props and consistent light. Include only a board or numerical visualization when it helps the viewer understand the subject.
3. If the story has a cast, develop it; character-free videos do not require one. People can have simple dark circular heads, readable face marks, individual clothes and jointed limbs; choose original designs suitable for culture, era and the topic. For animals, develop a species-appropriate silhouette, eyes, head/body/limb articulation, walk, turn and key behaviors. A quadruped needs four legs and appropriate weight transfer; a bird has head, body, wing and leg pivots. Do not reuse one human puppet unchanged as every animal.
4. Assign each scene a wide establishing shot, a purposeful medium/detail crop, and a fallback still pose. Use foreground/midground/background layers. Character position and gaze should direct attention to the active prop, partner or goal. Keep identity, silhouette and proportions consistent.
5. Animate a few meaningful actions: walking toward a goal, pointing, passing a prop, turning to react, a physical attempt, and a decisive change. Use a clear contact pose for object interaction. Contrast holds with focused motion; let scenes breathe. If the story involves complex physical action, use additional key poses and check anatomy/contact, or use a different framing that tells the story clearly with the available rig.
6. Add original pop-ups only when useful: brief labels for names, dates, steps, stakes, fictional amounts or comparisons; always label hypothetical values. A fictional story may use none. Show enough time for reading; do not crowd the scene.
7. Make the thumbnail's single visual promise match the script, regardless of niche. One protagonist or important object, one conflict, a simple background and 2–5 large optional words are useful starting points, not mandatory rules.

## 5. Voice, sound and pacing by intent

The example audio was not auditioned; all voice directions are original proposals. **Every spoken voice must use an American accent**, including characters. Use U.S. English (`en-US`) for English speech. Keep every voice energetic, confidently projected, clear and expressive rather than flat or boring; keep speech prominent over music and avoid shouting or clipping. Choose the niche-specific voice profile, detailed delivery, and example scripts in `voice-direction.md`: friendly and measured for a tutorial; steady and empathetic for serious biography; composed and qualified for finance; playful for comedy; distinct but non-stereotyped for fiction. Adapt pitch, gender presentation, emotion, language, and accessibility without changing the American-accent requirement. If an appropriate voice or accent cannot be verified, disclose that and deliver written script rather than silently using a different accent. Pacing should leave room for jokes, emotional moments, and on-screen reading. Use original or licensed sound, and never imply the reference has a particular soundtrack unless directly verified.

## 6. Quality gate adapted to the niche

- Is the topic recognizable within the opening without needing unrelated science imagery? Does the premise and visual world fit the audience?
- Is the chosen narrative complete for its form: lesson demonstrated, story resolved, argument supported, or question answered? If intentionally episodic, is the episode's promise still paid off?
- Are cast and props specific to the niche and consistent? If an animal or historical figure appears, are movements and essential characteristics appropriate and respectful?
- Are any visual numbers labeled as fictional examples where applicable? Are factual claims, dates, quotations, cultural references, safety instructions and risk statements verified for the relevant jurisdiction and time?
- Does motion show physical causality and clear intent rather than constant idle? Are captions, labels and dialogue readable and synchronized?
- Do the art, voice and music fit the requested mood? Can the viewer understand the story without borrowing the reference's audio, images or wording?
- Does the title/thumbnail match the video? Is a playlist recommendation based on the user's actual channel or conditional if unknown?
